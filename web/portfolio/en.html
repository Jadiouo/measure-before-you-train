1What I did

Measure first, then decide whether to train

I built a drone-formation simulation
to answer one question:
how much better than a simple rule can a trained policy do?

In formation flight, each drone behind the leader saves energy by riding the wake of the drone ahead; the leader gets no such help. The first empty battery ends the mission, so who leads, and when the lead changes hands, decides how far the team flies.

The result first: across the 960 settings of the basic and richer models, a simple rule that reads battery levels is within 2% of the best plan in 952. With neighbours only, a gap remains in 23 selected settings; the learner set-up I tried has not yet qualified to be tested on it.

All simulation, no hardware. Most code and experiments were done by AI coding agents under my direction.

2

The method

Guess first, measure, and make the learner pass a qualifying check

When a training run fails, you cannot tell whether there was no room to improve or the training was simply poor. So I measure the room first.

StepNameWhat it is
1Write the guess downBefore measuring, write my guess down and save it. Wrong guesses are reported too. The order rests on my own version-control record, not on third-party proof.
2Measure the gapScore the simple rule, then score the best plan achievable with hindsight (the paper's upper bound). The difference is the headroom. If it is under 2%, I judge training not worth trying.
3Sanity gateBefore a learner is tested, a better-informed version of it is tried on three control settings with a known gap. It must recover at least half of that gap in at least two of them. If it does not, the learner's results are not counted.

The 2% line is my own choice, made before measuring, not a derived quantity.

3

Three findings

One leaves almost no room, one supports only a narrow conclusion, one still has a gap

The simulation has three tasks: leader rotation; wake surfing (a follower flies in the wake of the drone ahead); and soaring (gliders announce rising air to one another).

3.1 A simple rule that reads battery levels leaves almost no room

The rule “give the lead to the drone with the most charge” is more than 2% from the best plan in only 8 of 960 leader-rotation settings, counting the basic and richer models. In the basic model, a time rule that hands the lead on at fixed intervals is often far off.

3.2 Against stronger references there is a gap, but the conclusion is narrower

The wake-surfing and soaring tasks have no best plan to compare with, only stronger reference controllers that are given extra information. Their lead exceeds 2 percentage points in some wake-surfing settings (against one of the two references only) and in most soaring settings. But a reference is not an upper bound, so this does not show that a learner has room, nor that it has none.

3.3 Seeing only neighbours still leaves a gap

When each drone sees only its neighbours' batteries (in noisy settings), the best rule trails the whole-team rule by a median of 1.41% of the best plan, across 23 settings.

4

Guess vs. result

Some of my guesses were right, some were wrong

Every item was guessed first and then measured. Here are the five main ones.

ItemWhat I guessedWhat happenedResult
Basic rotationWhen swapping is cheap, a rule that reads battery levels stays within 2 to 3% of the best planOnly 4 of 192 settings beyond 2%Right
Richer rotationAbout 30% of settings leave more than 2% of roomOnly 1 of 768 (the best of five rules)Wrong
Wake surfingGaps above 2 percentage points only when the wake drifts a lot, not when it drifts littleAs guessed, none at small drift; yet every gap above 2 pp came when the lead aircraft manoeuvredHalf right
SoaringA simple rule given the real data recovers most of the gapMost was recovered; the remaining lead was never confidently above 2 ppRight
Learner sanity gateA 75% chance of passing on the first tryThe first try failed; the second passed one control setting, where two were requiredWrong

The biggest miss was the richer leader rotation. I expected room where the drones differ a lot. None of those settings went beyond 2%, and the one setting that did is not among them.

My reading (untested): when drones differ a lot, putting the weakest one where it saves most gets close to the best, and a simple rule does that easily.

What each one taught is in the table in Section 4 of the paper. The statistics for all guesses are in the paper's appendix; some of them were not blind but written after earlier or partial results.

5

Still open

Can a learner close the neighbours-only gap? I do not know

A learner must first pass the sanity gate. I tried a better-informed version of it on three control settings. It passed one, and two were required. Following the rules written in advance, the real training was not run.

Without the gate, a failed training run could be read as “learning cannot close this gap”. The correct reading is that this learner setting cannot yet recover a gap that a rule is known to recover, so it says nothing about the original question.

Main limits

  • Everything is simulated, and the physics is assumed, not measured.
  • The 2% line is my own choice. With 1%, some conclusions change.
  • This check can say a learner adds little over a rule. It cannot say learning has no other value.
